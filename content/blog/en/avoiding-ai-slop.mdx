Specific references, explicit constraints and deliberate choices can make AI-generated interfaces less generic. This article organizes seven prompt techniques to try; their effectiveness should be checked in your own project.

## 1. Anchor in a specific reference, not "modern"

The single biggest lever is replacing "modern" with a name. A specific
brand, a specific magazine spread, a specific era. The model has crisp
representations of *Linear's product marketing*, of *2003 Wired editorial
design*, of *Swiss International Style posters*. It only has a fuzzy blob
labeled "modern web."

<Callout tone="info" title="Try this">
  Instead of "make it modern and clean," try "treat it like a Linear product
  page from late 2024 — strong sans-serif headings, generous negative space,
  thin separators, almost no color until the CTA."
</Callout>

## 2. Forbid the tells out loud

The model will not stop using `Inter` and purple gradients on its own. You
have to write down the things you do not want. A short prohibition list at
the top of the prompt does more than three paragraphs of positive direction.

<CodeBlock
  title="Forbidden list"
  language="text"
  code={`Forbidden:
- Inter, Roboto, Open Sans
- Purple-to-indigo gradients
- Three-up feature card rows on white
- The phrase "transform your workflow"
- Soft drop shadows (use hard 1px borders instead)`}
/>

## 4. Demand one strong visual decision per screen

Average designs are average because nothing dares to be the loudest thing
in the room. Ask explicitly for the one thing that should be the boldest
on the page, and tell the model that everything else has to recede.

> The hero headline is set in 92px display serif. Everything else on the
> page — body, buttons, separators — is set at 14–16px in a neutral
> sans-serif. The headline is the only thing competing for attention.

This single instruction does more for visual hierarchy than any amount of
"make sure the headline stands out."

## 5. Specify the spatial system, not just the colour system

People obsess over palette and forget that 80% of how an interface *feels*
is spacing, rhythm, and where the alignment lines are. A simple way to
push the model out of slop is to mandate the grid.

<CodeBlock
  title="Spatial directives"
  language="text"
  code={`Layout:
- 12-column grid, 80px gutters at desktop
- Hero content sits in columns 2-8 (asymmetric, not centred)
- Body rhythm: 8px base, all vertical spacing is a multiple of 24px
- Section dividers are 1px solid rgba(0,0,0,0.08), never a shadow`}
/>

## 6. Give the interface a voice, not a feature list

A lot of AI-generated copy reads like a feature spec, which is then visually
reinforced by a grid of feature cards. Decide on a tone first — *terse and
confident*, *playful and self-deprecating*, *clinical and data-led* — and
ask the model to write headlines in that voice **before** it lays anything
out. The layout then follows the voice instead of dictating it.

## 7. Iterate by deleting

The fastest way to fix a slopped-out screen is not to add more direction —
it is to delete elements. After the first generation, look at the output
and identify the three things that scream "AI did this." Then prompt:

> Remove the gradient hero background, remove the three-up feature row,
> remove the testimonial card. Replace each with empty space. Keep
> iterating until the page can survive having things removed.

What is left after that pruning is usually the bones of something good.
Anything you can delete without missing it was slop in the first place.

## The meta-rule

Every one of these techniques is the same idea in a different costume:
**replace generality with a decision**. The model will happily generate
the average of the web for you. Your job in the prompt is to refuse the
average — by name, by negation, by curated reference, by explicit
constraint.

If you want output that looks like a designer made it, you have to do the
designer's job *in the prompt*: pick the style, pick the type, pick the
palette, pick the one loud thing. The Skill exists to make picking from
a curated set the path of least resistance, so you do not have to write
those decisions from scratch every time.
