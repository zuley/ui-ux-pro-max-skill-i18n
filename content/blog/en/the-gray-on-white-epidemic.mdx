We wrote earlier about [dark mode that is actually dark](/blog/dark-mode-thats-actually-dark).
This is the companion piece, and it is the one with the higher body
count: **light mode is where generated UIs actually fail contrast**,
because the failure photographs well.

The pattern is always the same. The model wants visual hierarchy, and
the cheapest hierarchy is opacity: headline in near-black, everything
"secondary" in a tasteful gray. One step of secondary is fine. But
generated pages apply it recursively — secondary of the secondary —
and by the third level your pricing caveats are `#9CA3AF` on white.
That is a 2.5:1 ratio. The WCAG AA floor for body text is **4.5:1**.

It looks refined in the screenshot. On a phone, outdoors, at 60%
brightness, it is not text anymore. It is texture.

## The four crime scenes

Audit a generated landing page and the failures cluster in the same
four places, every time:

- **The subheadline.** The single most-read sentence on the page after
  the headline, and the model styles it `text-gray-400` because it is
  "supporting copy". The most important words are the lightest ink.
- **Chart labels and axes.** Data is the credibility section, and its
  numbers are routinely set at 11px in light gray — doubly degraded.
- **Placeholder and helper text in forms.** Often styled *identically*
  to disabled states, so users cannot tell an empty field from a dead one.
- **The footer.** Links people actually need — pricing, docs, contact —
  at 3:1 because the footer is treated as decoration.

The common thread: the model equates *importance* with *ink*, and it
ranks importance by section type, not by what the reader needs. A
pricing caveat is legally load-bearing; it gets the lightest gray on
the page anyway.

## The arithmetic nobody runs

Contrast ratios are not intuitive, which is why "it looks fine to me"
fails. On white, the popular grays land here:

<CodeBlock
  title="Tailwind grays on a white background"
  language="text"
  code={`gray-300  #D1D5DB   1.5:1  — decorative only, never text
gray-400  #9CA3AF   2.5:1  — fails everything, ships constantly
gray-500  #6B7280   4.8:1  — passes on pure white; 4.4:1 and failing on a gray-100 card
gray-600  #4B5563   7.6:1  — passes everywhere, with margin. The safe floor.
gray-700  #374151  10.3:1  — comfortable body ink`}
/>

The whole epidemic is the gap between `gray-400` and `gray-600`. Two
steps on a Tailwind scale, the difference between texture and text.

Note the trap in `gray-500`: it clears 4.5:1 on pure white — and loses
that margin the moment the background tints. On a `gray-100` card it is
already failing, and tinted cards are exactly where generated layouts
love to put secondary text. Treat it as a large-text color (18px+ bold,
24px+ regular) and you stop having to audit every background it lands on.

## Fixing it at the prompt, not in review

Catching this in review means re-rolling styled components one at a
time. Cheaper to make it impossible upfront. Two blocks do it:

<CodeBlock
  title="Ink policy block for UI prompts"
  language="text"
  code={`# Text colors (light mode — exhaustive list, nothing else)
Primary ink:   #1F2937 — headlines, body, anything multi-line
Secondary ink: #4B5563 — captions, labels, helper text, footer links
Accent ink:    <your accent, only if it measures 4.5:1 on white>

Rules:
- No text node may use any other color, including via opacity utilities.
- Nothing below 4.5:1 against its actual background. If unsure, darker.
- Placeholder text uses secondary ink, not a lighter shade.
- Disabled states may break these rules — and must also be marked
  disabled semantically, not just visually.`}
/>

Two ink levels, not three. Every level you add is an invitation to
recurse back down to `gray-400`. We have yet to find a marketing page
that needed a third level of gray rather than better copy.

<Callout tone="warn" title="Watch for opacity laundering">
  Models also produce light gray as white at 60% opacity, or
  `text-gray-900/50`. Same ratio, invisible to a color-token grep —
  which is why the rule above says "including via opacity utilities."
  Audit computed styles, not class names.
</Callout>

## The two-minute audit

For pages that already exist: open devtools, sample these four spots
against their real backgrounds — subheadline, smallest chart label,
form helper text, footer link. Any contrast checker works. If those
four pass at 4.5:1, the rest of the page almost always does; they are
the canaries because they are where "secondary of the secondary"
bottoms out.

Hierarchy is real, and gray is a legitimate tool for it. The fix is not
"make everything black" — it is capping the recursion at two named ink
levels, both of which clear the floor. Decide the inks once, write them
into the prompt, and light mode stops being the mode where your most
careful readers squint.
