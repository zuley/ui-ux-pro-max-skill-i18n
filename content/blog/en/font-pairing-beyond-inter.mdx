Choose typography by product voice, heading/body contrast and practical reading requirements. These three questions are learning guidance, not a claim that this site designed the upstream font catalog.

## Question 1: What is the voice of this product?

Not the audience — the *voice*. Write one sentence as if the product
could talk: "I am precise and slightly stern." "I am warm and hand-made."
"I am expensive and quiet." That sentence maps to typography more
directly than any demographic does:

- **Precise / technical** → a grotesque or geometric sans for headings
  (Space Grotesk, IBM Plex Sans), often with a mono accent.
- **Warm / human** → a humanist sans (Source Sans 3, Nunito Sans) or a
  low-contrast serif for body (Source Serif 4).
- **Expensive / editorial** → a high-contrast display serif for
  headings (Fraunces, Playfair Display) over a restrained sans body.
- **Playful / consumer** → rounded terminals (Quicksand, Baloo 2) —
  and discipline everywhere else, or it tips into a toy.

If you cannot write the voice sentence, stop. That is not a typography
problem yet; it is a positioning problem, and no pairing will fix it.

## Question 2: Where is the contrast between heading and body?

A pairing works when the two faces differ on **exactly one obvious
axis** and agree on everything else. Serif display over sans body:
contrast of *construction*. Bold grotesque over quiet humanist:
contrast of *temperature*. Mono headings over sans body: contrast of
*context* — machine voice above, human voice below.

Pairs fail in two symmetrical ways. **Too close** (Inter + Roboto):
the reader registers a vague inconsistency, like two whites that almost
match. **Too far** (brush script + geometric sans): the axes multiply
and the page reads as two products stapled together.

<Callout tone="info" title="The squint test">
  Set a heading over two paragraphs, step back, squint. You should
  instantly tell heading from body (contrast exists), and they should
  still look like they were bought as a set (one axis, not three).
</Callout>

## Question 3: Does the body face survive 16px on a mediocre screen?

Display faces are auditioned at 64px, body faces live at 14–16px. The
checklist for body duty is short: a generous x-height, open apertures
(the `e` and `a` do not close up), real italics if you will ever
emphasise, and tabular figures if numbers matter to your product.
Fraunces is a gorgeous heading face and an unreadable body face — most
"failed pairings" are actually a display face pressed into body duty.

## Encoding the choice into a prompt

A name alone will not survive contact with the model — it will load
Playfair and still set your buttons in Inter, because you never said
otherwise. Decisions, then constraints:

<CodeBlock
  title="Typography block for a UI prompt"
  language="text"
  code={`# Typography (locked)
Headings: Fraunces, weight 600, tight leading (1.1), only at h1/h2.
Body + UI controls: Source Sans 3, 16px base, leading 1.6.
Numbers in the pricing table: tabular figures.
Forbidden: Inter, Roboto, Open Sans, system-ui as a "fallback choice",
any third typeface anywhere.`}
/>

The forbidden list matters more than the positive list. "Use Fraunces"
plus silence still leaves the model its habits; "no third typeface
anywhere" is the line that actually changes the output.

## Three pairings to consider

From the Skill's library, with their voice sentences:

- **Space Grotesk / Source Sans 3** — "I am a sharp tool, not a
  platform." Dev tools, analytics, anything with a terminal in its DNA.
- **Fraunces / Source Sans 3** — "I am considered and a little
  literary." Editorial products, premium services, brands that say
  *taste*.
- **IBM Plex Sans / IBM Plex Mono (accent)** — "I am infrastructure you
  can trust." Fintech, security, B2B that wants gravitas without a suit.

None of these is exotic. That is the point: the win over Inter-everywhere
was never obscurity — it is that someone made a decision, and the page
shows it.
