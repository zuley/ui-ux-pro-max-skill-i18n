Of the 50 styles the Skill ships, three get requested by name more
than all the others combined: glassmorphism, neumorphism, and flat.
They get requested as *moods* — "make it glassy" — but they are not
moods. Each one is a bundle of engineering trade-offs in contrast,
performance, and maintenance cost. Choosing between them by vibe is
how a page ends up paying glassmorphism prices for a flat-design job.

Here is each one as a decision, not a look.

## Glassmorphism: rented depth, paid for in blur

Frosted translucency, a thin light border, background bleeding through
a `backdrop-filter: blur()`. It signals *layered, modern, premium* —
and it has two real costs everyone discovers late:

- **Contrast becomes a moving target.** Text on glass sits on whatever
  happens to scroll underneath. Your 4.5:1 against the resting state
  can drop to 2:1 the moment a photo slides behind the panel.
- **`backdrop-filter` is one of the most expensive properties in CSS.**
  A glass navbar over a scrolling page repaints the blur every frame —
  on mid-range phones this is the difference between 60fps and 35.

The discipline that keeps it working: glass on **at most two element
types** (typically the navbar and one floating panel), never on
anything containing body text, and always with a solid-color fallback
via `@supports`. Glassmorphism is a garnish. Plated as a main course,
it is unreadable and slow.

## Neumorphism: the style that ate its own affordances

Soft extruded shapes, same-color backgrounds, identity expressed
entirely through paired light/dark shadows. It photographs beautifully,
which is why it keeps coming back — and it has a structural problem no
palette can fix: **its whole identity lives below 3:1 contrast.** The
moment every edge passes WCAG, it stops being neumorphism.

That is not a reason to ban it. It is a reason to scope it: decorative
surfaces only — cards, players, dashboard tiles — while every
*interactive* element (buttons, toggles, inputs) gets a real border or
fill on top of the soft shadows. Pure neumorphic buttons present
pressed and unpressed as nearly identical states; users with low vision
simply cannot operate them. If the product is utilitarian and
information-dense, this style is fighting you on every screen.

## Flat: the default that needs a reason to be left

No simulated depth; hierarchy from type scale, spacing, and color
blocks alone. Cheapest to render, easiest to keep consistent, trivially
themeable. The honest framing: **flat is the default, and the other
two are deviations you should be able to justify in one sentence.**
"Because it looked plain" is not that sentence — plainness in flat
design is almost always a typography problem wearing a style costume.
Before reaching for blur or shadows, try one size up on the type
contrast and one accent color used with actual discipline.

## The decision table

  title="Choosing between the three"

  code={`Question                            glass   neumorph   flat
Body text sits on it?                no      ok         ok
Information-dense dashboard?         no      no         yes
Marketing page, premium feel?        yes     careful    yes
Must hit WCAG AA everywhere?         hard    very hard  easy
Low-end mobile traffic matters?      no      ok         yes
Team without a design reviewer?      no      no         yes`}
/>

Two patterns fall out of the table. Flat is never the wrong answer —
sometimes just an under-seasoned one. And the two depth styles are
*page-level* choices, not system-level ones: a glass navbar over an
otherwise flat system is coherent; a whole product built on glass is a
performance bug with a mood.

## Saying it in a prompt

Style names alone under-constrain — "glassmorphism" to a model means
*put blur on everything*. Name the style, then fence it:

<CodeBlock
  title="Style block with a fence"
  language="text"
  code={`# Style: Glassmorphism, scoped
- Glass treatment: navbar and the floating stats panel ONLY.
- Everything else: flat surfaces, solid backgrounds, 1px borders.
- No body text on glass. Labels on glass: 600 weight minimum, and
  verify 4.5:1 against the LIGHTEST region of the page background.
- Provide an @supports fallback: solid bg at 95% opacity, no blur.`}
/>

The fence is the part the model will not supply on its own, and it is
also the part a design reviewer would have insisted on. Styles are not
costumes a page wears; they are budgets a page spends. Pick the one
whose costs land where your product can afford them.
